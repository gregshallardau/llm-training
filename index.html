Module 00: Think of It as a Brain

🧠 The brilliant new colleague on their permanent first day: vast knowledge, zero memory of you, desperate to help.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 00

Plain English

"The AI is built on human knowledge, so it works a lot like a brain — it reads in chunks, focuses on what matters, builds sentences one word at a time, and makes stuff up when it doesn't know. The biggest difference? It has amnesia. Every conversation is day one. Your job is to write the onboarding document that makes day one productive."

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 00

The One Analogy to Rule Them All

The LLM is a brilliant new team member on their permanent first day.

They have:

  • Read every document in the industry
  • A photographic memory for patterns in language
  • The ability to write at extraordinary speed
  • Zero knowledge of YOU, your clients, your team, or your preferences

And every morning, they wake up with total amnesia. They've never met you before. They don't know what you worked on yesterday. They don't know your clients' names, your writing style, or what "remark" means at your firm.

That's why the base layer exists. It's the onboarding document you hand them every morning. The better that document is, the better every interaction will be — not just the first one, but all of them.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 00

Technical

"The transformer architecture implements functional analogues of human cognitive processes — chunked perception (tokenisation/BPE), selective attention (self-attention/QK^T), working memory constraints (context window), and sequential production (autoregressive generation). The key architectural gap is the absence of episodic memory — there's no hippocampal analogue for cross-session persistence. The base layer is an external memory prosthetic that compensates for this gap."

PhD ▼▼ Module 00

PhD Track

"The parallels between transformer cognition and human cognition are both illuminating and misleading. Attention mechanisms approximate the selective attention studied by Treisman (1964) and Posner (1980), with multi-head attention corresponding to feature-based attention streams. The context window functions as a bounded working memory (cf. Cowan's 4±1 capacity, scaled by the architectural embedding dimension). Generation is sequential like speech production (Levelt's model), with each token choice conditioned on the full context — analogous to the interaction of formulation and articulation stages. However, the absence of a learning mechanism at inference time (no synaptic plasticity analogue) means the model cannot form new associations within a session — it can only leverage associations learned during training. The base layer compensates by converting the user's private knowledge into in-context information, effectively substituting external episodic memory for the missing internal mechanism."

Module 01: What is an LLM?

🧠 Predictive text / autocomplete: your phone's next-word guess — but it read the whole internet.

❓ What am I actually talking to?

An LLM is a program that predicts the most likely next word, over and over, until it's written a full response.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 01

Plain English

Imagine you had a friend who had read every book, every website, every email, every document ever written in English. You start a sentence, and they finish it — not by copying something they read, but by having absorbed the patterns of how language works.

"The project is currently running two weeks behind schedule due to..." → your friend knows the next word is probably a reason — "delays", "weather", "supply chain" — because that's how thousands of similar sentences end.

An LLM is that friend, except it's a computer program, and it's read roughly 10 trillion words. It doesn't "understand" anything the way you do. It has an extraordinarily sophisticated sense of "what word usually comes next in this context."

The key insight: It's not searching a database. It's not looking up answers. It's generating text one word at a time based on patterns.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 01

Technical

A Large Language Model is a neural network — specifically a transformer architecture — trained on massive text corpora (Common Crawl, Wikipedia, books, code, forums, etc.) to perform next-token prediction.

The training process:

  1. Show the model a sequence of tokens (words/word-pieces)
  2. Ask it to predict the next token
  3. Compare its prediction to the actual next token
  4. Adjust the model's parameters (weights) to reduce the error
  5. Repeat this billions of times across trillions of tokens

The result is a model with billions of parameters (numerical weights) that encode statistical patterns of language. GPT-4 has ~1.8 trillion parameters. Claude has an undisclosed number, estimated in the hundreds of billions.

Key components:

  • Parameters (weights): The numbers that define the model. Learned during training. Fixed after training.
  • Architecture: The transformer — a specific way of wiring the neural network that uses self-attention to process all tokens in parallel rather than sequentially.
  • Training data: The text corpus. Quality and breadth of training data is one of the biggest differentiators between models.
  • Fine-tuning / RLHF: After base training, models are further trained on human feedback to be helpful, harmless, and honest.
PhD ▼▼ Module 01

PhD Track

The transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017, "Attention Is All You Need") replaced recurrent architectures (RNNs, LSTMs) by eliminating sequential dependency in processing.

A transformer-based LLM computes:

P(x_t | x_1, x_2, ..., x_{t-1}) = softmax(f_θ(x_1, ..., x_{t-1}))

Where f_θ is the transformer function parameterised by θ (the learned weights), and the output is a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary V for position t.

Training objective is to minimise the cross-entropy loss over the training corpus:

L(θ) = -∑_t log P_θ(x_t | x_{<t})

This is equivalent to maximising the likelihood of the training data under the model — the model learns the statistical structure of language by being forced to predict it.

Scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020) showed that model performance improves predictably as a power law with three factors:

  • Number of parameters (N)
  • Size of training dataset (D)
  • Amount of compute used for training (C)
L(N) ∝ N^{-0.076}

This means you need roughly 10× more parameters to halve the loss. This relationship held over 7 orders of magnitude, which is why the industry raced to build ever-larger models — the returns were mathematically predictable.

Module 01b

Module 01b: How an LLM Is Trained

🧠 Raising and schooling a person: it *reads the whole library* (pretraining), does *an apprenticeship* (fine-tuning), then *learns manners from feedback* (RLHF). Three stages, same as bringing up a bright kid.

❓ Where does it get its knowledge?

An LLM is built in three stages: first it reads a vast chunk of the internet and drills one skill — *predict the next word* (**pretraining**); then it's shown examples of following instructions (**fine-tuning**); then it's shaped by human thumbs-up / thumbs-down until it's helpful and safe (**RLHF**). By the end, everything it "knows" is frozen into billions of numbers called **weights** — and then the reading stops.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 01b

Plain English (1/2)

Imagine locking a brilliant, fast reader in the world's biggest library and giving them one game, played a trillion times: cover the next word and guess it.

"The client's insurance policy expires on June ___"

Guess "30th." Check. Wrong? Adjust. Right? Reinforce. Do that across essentially the whole public internet — books, websites, code, forums — and something remarkable happens: to get good at guessing the next word, the reader is forced to learn grammar, facts, reasoning, and the patterns of how the world is described. Nobody teaches it insurance; it just read millions of pages where insurance came up, and the patterns stuck.

That's the whole trick. Predicting the next word, at enormous scale, is how it accidentally learns everything. It's not memorising the library word-for-word — it's absorbing the patterns of the library into its "brain."

Two things this explains straight away:

  • The knowledge cutoff. The reading stops at some date. Ask about anything after that and it's genuinely never seen it — like a colleague who's been on a desert island since January.
  • Why it reflects its data. If the library leaned a certain way, so will the reader. Its knowledge is its reading.

The base model is a brilliant graduate who's read everything but has never had a job. Fine-tuning is the apprenticeship: you sit them down with thousands of worked examples of "here's a request, here's a good response," and they learn the shape of being useful.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 01b

Plain English (2/2)

Request: "Summarise this policy in three bullet points."
Good response: "• Covers... • Excludes... • Expires..."

Show enough of these — questions answered, instructions followed, emails drafted — and the graduate stops continuing your text and starts helping you. Same knowledge as before; brand-new behaviour. This is where a raw predictor becomes an assistant.

The apprentice is now useful — but useful isn't the same as good. Two answers can both follow the instruction while one is clear, honest and safe and the other is waffly, smug, or reckless. Teaching that difference is RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, and it's exactly how you teach a person manners: feedback.

Humans are shown pairs of the model's answers and pick the better one — thousands of times. The model learns a sense of "this kind of answer gets a thumbs-up, that kind gets a thumbs-down," and shifts toward the thumbs-up. That's where the model's "personality" comes from — the helpfulness, the caution, the refusal to do harmful things. Not baked into the knowledge; taught by feedback afterwards.

This stage is also why the same underlying knowledge can feel very different between models — they were given different manners.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 01b

Technical

Pretraining is self-supervised learning on a next-token-prediction objective — no human labels needed, because the "answer" is just the next token already in the text.

The loop, repeated across trillions of tokens:

  1. Feed the model a sequence; ask it to predict the next token.
  2. Compare its predicted probability distribution to the actual next token.
  3. Compute the loss (how wrong it was — cross-entropy).
  4. Backpropagation + gradient descent: nudge every weight slightly in the direction that would have made the right answer more likely.
  5. Repeat billions of times.

Scale is the story:

  • Data: trillions of tokens (a large fraction of the crawlable web, plus books and code).
  • Parameters (weights): billions to hundreds of billions of numbers.
  • Compute: thousands of GPUs for weeks–months; the expensive part of the whole pipeline.

The output of this stage is a base model — fluent, knowledgeable, but not helpful. It completes text; it doesn't yet answer questions. Ask a raw base model "What is a binder?" and it might reply with more exam questions, because internet text often lists questions together. It has the knowledge but not the behaviour.

PhD ▼▼ Module 01b

PhD Track

Objective: minimise the cross-entropy of the next-token distribution,

$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$, over the corpus.

  • Scaling laws (Kaplan 2020; Chinchilla, Hoffmann 2022): loss falls as a power law in parameters, data, and compute. Chinchilla showed most large models were under-trained on data — compute-optimal training balances parameters and tokens (~20 tokens per parameter), which is why "smaller model, more data" often wins.
  • Optimisation: AdamW, cosine LR schedules, warmup, gradient clipping; mixed-precision (bf16); data/tensor/pipeline parallelism across the cluster.
  • Emergence: capabilities (in-context learning, chain-of-thought) that are weak or absent in small models appear at scale — still an active research debate (real phase change vs. artefact of the metric).
  • Key reading: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3, Brown 2020); Training Compute-Optimal LLMs (Chinchilla).

Module 02: Tokenisation

🧠 Arcade tokens: the machine only runs on tokens; text is chopped into little coins to feed it.

❓ How does it read my words?

Before an LLM can do anything with your text, it chops it into small pieces called **tokens** — roughly ¾ of a word each — and converts each piece into a number. The model never sees words. It sees numbers.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 02

Plain English

You type: "The client's property insurance policy expires on June 30, 2026."

The model doesn't read that as words. It reads it as chunks:

[The] [client] ['s] [property] [insurance] [policy] [expires] [on] [June] [ 30] [,] [ 2026] [.]

That's 13 tokens. Each one gets a unique number (like a barcode). The model works entirely in these numbers — it never "sees" the letters.

Why does this matter to you? Two reasons:

  1. There's a limit. Your Copilot can only hold a certain number of tokens at once (like a desk that can only hold so many papers). If you paste in a 50-page document, some of it falls off the desk.
  2. Uncommon words get chopped more. "Insurance" is one token. "Reinsurance" might be two: [Re] [insurance]. Jargon and unusual terms cost more tokens — the model has to work harder to process them.

Rule of thumb: 1 token ≈ ¾ of a word. Or: 100 words ≈ 130 tokens. A page of text ≈ 500 tokens.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 02

Technical

Tokenisation is the process of converting raw text into a sequence of integer IDs from a fixed vocabulary. Modern LLMs use Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) or variants (SentencePiece, WordPiece).

How BPE works:

  1. Start with the raw text as individual bytes (characters)
  2. Count every adjacent pair of bytes in the training corpus
  3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new single token
  4. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size (typically 32K–100K tokens)

Example of the merge process:

Step 0: [t] [h] [e]                     → frequency of "th" is very high
Step 1: [th] [e]                         → "the" is common
Step 2: [the]                            → single token

The result is a vocabulary where:

  • Common words = 1 token ("the", "insurance", "client")
  • Less common words = 2-3 tokens ("re" + "insurance", "under" + "writ" + "er")
  • Rare words or misspellings = many tokens (character-level fallback)
  • Numbers = complex (each digit or pair of digits may be a separate token)
  • Whitespace and punctuation = their own tokens

Vocabulary sizes:

  • GPT-4: ~100K tokens
  • Claude: undisclosed, estimated 100K+
  • Llama: 32K tokens

Implications for prompting:

  • Dense, well-structured prompts use fewer tokens than rambling ones
  • Technical jargon doesn't "confuse" the model but does consume more tokens
  • The tokenizer is trained on the same corpus as the model — common patterns in the training data tokenise efficiently
PhD ▼▼ Module 02

PhD Track

BPE (Sennrich et al., 2016) is a compression algorithm applied to language. The vocabulary is optimised to minimise the total token count across the training corpus — a compression objective:

V* = argmin_V ∑_{d ∈ D} |tokenize_V(d)|

Where D is the training corpus and V is the vocabulary. This means the tokenizer learns a vocabulary that efficiently encodes the statistical structure of the training text.

The resulting tokenization has properties analogous to Huffman coding — frequent sequences get shorter representations, rare sequences get longer ones. The difference is that BPE operates on subword units rather than characters, and the "code" is a fixed-width integer (the token ID) rather than a variable-length bit string.

Token embeddings: Each token ID maps to a learned vector in ℝ^d (where d is the model dimension, typically 4096–12288). These embeddings are the model's "understanding" of each token — tokens that appear in similar contexts will have similar embeddings (distributional hypothesis, Harris 1954).

embedding(token_id) → vector ∈ ℝ^d

The embedding matrix E ∈ ℝ^{|V| × d} is learned during training. It's the first transformation: raw text → token IDs → dense vectors. Everything the model does from this point operates on these vectors, not the text.

Module 03

Module 03: The Attention Mechanism

🧠 A DJ's mixing desk: sliders deciding how *loud* each word is in the mix.

❓ How does it decide what matters?

The attention mechanism lets every word in your prompt "look at" every other word to figure out which ones are relevant to each other — so when the model is writing about "the client's liability exposure," it knows to pay attention to the claims history you mentioned three paragraphs ago.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 03

Plain English

Imagine you're reading a long client file. Your eyes don't read every word with equal focus. When you see "claims history," your brain automatically jumps back to the claims table you saw earlier. When you see "the client," your brain links it to the name at the top of the document.

That's what attention does. For every word, the model asks: "Which other words in this document are most relevant to me right now?" Then it focuses on those words more than the irrelevant ones.

The library analogy (from Module 01, expanded):

You walk into a library with a question in mind. You scan the spines of every book simultaneously (not one by one — simultaneously). Some spines light up because they're relevant to your question. You pull those books and blend their contents into your answer.

Now imagine you're not just one person — you're 96 people, each with a slightly different question, all searching the library at the same time. One person is looking for "what industry is this client in?" Another is looking for "what's the emotional tone of this text?" Another is looking for "are there numbers nearby that might be a dollar amount?"

That's multi-head attention. The model runs multiple parallel attention searches, each looking for a different type of relationship. The combined result is a rich understanding of how every word relates to every other word.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 03

Technical

Attention is the core mechanism of the transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017). For every token in the sequence, it computes three vectors:

  • Q (Query): "What am I looking for?" — what information does this token need?
  • K (Key): "What do I contain?" — what information does this token advertise?
  • V (Value): "Here's my content." — the actual information to be passed along if there's a match.

The attention computation:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / √d_k) × V

Step by step:

  1. QK^T — dot product of every Query with every Key. Produces a score matrix. High score = "these two tokens are relevant to each other."
  2. / √d_k — scale by the square root of the key dimension. Prevents the dot products from becoming too large (which would push the softmax into regions where gradients vanish).
  3. softmax — normalise each row so the scores sum to 1. Now they're weights — "pay 40% attention to this word, 20% to that one, etc."
  4. × V — use those weights to blend the Value vectors. Each token's output is a weighted average of all Values, weighted by how relevant each token is.

PhD ▼▼ Module 03

PhD Track

The scaled dot-product attention can be understood through the lens of kernel methods and associative memory.

The attention matrix A = softmax(QK^T / √d_k) is a row-stochastic matrix — each row sums to 1, defining a probability distribution over the sequence. The output for position i is:

output_i = ∑_j A_{ij} V_j

This is a soft dictionary lookup — Q is the query, K are the keys, V are the values, and softmax provides a differentiable approximation of argmax (hard lookup).

Why √d_k? Without scaling, the expected magnitude of QK^T grows as O(d_k) assuming entries are drawn from N(0,1). For large d_k, the softmax saturates (one entry dominates), producing near-one-hot attention patterns that block gradient flow. The 1/√d_k scaling maintains unit variance in the dot products.

Attention as kernel regression: The attention mechanism is equivalent to Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression with a softmax kernel:

f(q) = ∑_j K(q, k_j) v_j / ∑_j K(q, k_j)
where K(q, k) = exp(q · k / √d_k)

This connects transformers to a well-studied area of nonparametric statistics.

Module 04: Context Windows

🧠 Dory (Finding Nemo): limited working memory — overflow the fishbowl and the early stuff is forgotten.

❓ How much can it hold in its head?

The context window is the model's working memory — the total amount of text it can "see" at once. Everything beyond that limit doesn't exist to the model.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 04

Plain English

Imagine you're writing a report, but you can only see one page of notes at a time. If all the information you need fits on that page, you'll write a great report. If the information is spread across 50 pages and you can only see one, you'll miss things.

That's the context window. It's the size of the model's "desk" — how many words it can look at simultaneously when writing its response.

Key numbers for your tools:

  • Microsoft Copilot: ~128K tokens (~96,000 words, ~200 pages)
  • GPT-4o (behind most company GPTs): ~128K tokens
  • Claude: 200K tokens

Sounds like a lot, right? Here's the catch:

Everything counts against the limit:

  • Your system prompt / base layer → tokens
  • The conversation history (every message back and forth) → tokens
  • Any documents you've pasted in → tokens
  • The model's response → tokens

So in a long Copilot conversation, you might have 20 back-and-forth messages + pasted documents + a system prompt, and you're already 40% through your context window before the latest question.

What happens when you hit the limit?

The model starts "forgetting" — older messages get dropped from the window. It's like papers sliding off the edge of your desk. The model literally cannot see them anymore. This is why Copilot sometimes seems to forget what you discussed 10 messages ago — it actually has.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 04

Technical

The context window is the maximum sequence length the model can process in a single forward pass. It's a hard architectural constraint determined during training.

Why it exists:

Self-attention computes relationships between ALL tokens in the sequence. The attention matrix is n × n (where n is the sequence length). This means:

  • Memory: O(n²) — storing all pairwise attention scores
  • Compute: O(n²d) — computing all dot products

For n = 128K:

  • Attention matrix: 128K × 128K = 16.4 billion entries per head per layer
  • With 96 heads and 128 layers: ~200 trillion attention computations per forward pass

This is why context windows have practical limits — doubling the context length quadruples the compute.

Module 04b: The Plumbing

🧠 Mario's warp pipes: the pipes under the floor, moving data from place to place.

❓ Why can't AI just read my files?

AI is only as good as the data you feed it — and most company data lives in formats that AI reads poorly, if at all.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 04b

Plain English (1/2)

AI reads plain text the way you read a clean printed page — quickly, accurately, no friction.

A PDF is like reading through frosted glass. The words are there, technically. But the structure is blurry. Tables get garbled. Headers disappear. Two-column layouts become one long stream of jumbled text. The AI can see something, but it can't tell a row header from a data cell.

A scanned PDF is even worse. The AI isn't reading text at all — it's looking at a photograph of text. That's like describing a spreadsheet to someone on the phone by reading out every pixel of the image, row by row.

Markdown is like reading a well-organised notebook. Clear headings. Bullet points. Tables with visible structure. The AI has seen billions of pages of markdown — every GitHub README, every software documentation page, every technical forum. It's the native language of the internet. When you give the AI markdown, you're speaking its first language.

The fix isn't complicated: convert to plain text or markdown before feeding to AI. Same information. Dramatically better results.

Think of it like passing a story through a game of telephone — but each person improves it instead of garbling it.

You start with the full story. You tell someone the key points (distil). They write it up properly (expand). You pull out the headline (distil). They put it in the right format (expand).

Plain English (2/2)

PhD ▼▼ Module 04b

PhD Track

The document format problem is a signal-to-noise problem in an information-theoretic sense.

Let the content of a document be a random variable C (the actual information: facts, structure, relationships between data). The raw file F (the .docx, .pdf, .pptx) is an encoding of C with additional noise N:

F = encode(C) + N

where N includes formatting markup, rendering instructions, layout data, embedded fonts, metadata, and structural artifacts. The token count of F is:

|tokens(F)| = |tokens(C)| + |tokens(N)|

The signal-to-noise ratio in tokens is |tokens(C)| / |tokens(F)|. For clean markdown, this approaches 1.0. For a raw .docx XML extraction, empirical measurements put it below 0.1 — more than 90% of tokens are noise.

This matters for more than efficiency. The attention mechanism (Module 03) weights every token against every other token. Noise tokens draw attention budget away from content tokens. A context window filled with formatting artifacts literally reduces the model's effective attention on the information that matters.

Shannon's source coding theorem (1948) establishes that the minimum description length of content C is its entropy H(C). A well-structured markdown representation approaches this minimum. Every excess token beyond H(C) is either redundancy or noise — and in a context window with a fixed size n, every noise token displaces a content token.

Module 05: How Generation Works

🧠 A jazz improviser: making up the next note one at a time, guided by what came before — never seeing the ending.

❓ How does it write its answers?

The model writes one token at a time, each time asking "given everything I've seen so far (your prompt + what I've written so far), what's the most likely next word?" — and then it picks one and repeats.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 05

Plain English

When Copilot writes a response, it's not pulling a pre-written answer from a database. It's building the response one word at a time, like this:

Step 1: "The" → what's the most likely next word? → "client"
Step 2: "The client" → next? → "'s"
Step 3: "The client's" → next? → "property"
Step 4: "The client's property" → next? → "insurance"
...and so on, hundreds of times, until it decides the response is complete.

At each step, it's not just picking the single most likely word. It's choosing from a probability distribution — a ranked list of possible next words with percentages:

After "The client's property insurance":
  "policy"    → 34%
  "program"   → 22%
  "coverage"  → 18%
  "premium"   → 8%
  "portfolio" → 5%
  ... thousands more options, each less likely

The model rolls the dice (weighted by those percentages) and picks one. This is why you get slightly different answers every time you ask the same question — the dice land differently.

Temperature controls how "creative" vs "predictable" the model is:

  • Low temperature (0.0): Always picks the most likely word. Predictable, repetitive, but safe.
  • High temperature (1.0+): More willing to pick less likely words. More creative, surprising, but also more likely to say something weird.

Most Copilot/GPT interactions use a moderate temperature — creative enough to be useful, constrained enough to be reliable.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 05

Technical

Text generation in autoregressive LLMs is an iterative process:

for each position t:
    1. Forward pass: compute P(x_t | x_1, ..., x_{t-1})
    2. Sample: draw x_t from this distribution
    3. Append x_t to the sequence
    4. Repeat until stop condition

The forward pass runs the entire transformer stack (embeddings → N layers of attention + feedforward → final projection) to produce a probability distribution over the vocabulary for the next position.

Sampling strategies:

StrategyHow it worksWhen to use
GreedyAlways pick argmaxDeterministic tasks (code, structured data)
Temperature samplingScale logits by 1/T before softmaxGeneral use — T < 1 for focused, T > 1 for creative
Top-kOnly consider the k most likely tokensPrevents very unlikely tokens
Top-p (nucleus)Only consider tokens whose cumulative probability ≤ pAdaptive — considers more tokens when uncertain, fewer when confident
Min-pOnly consider tokens with probability ≥ p × max_probabilityBetter than top-k at scaling with confidence

In practice, most APIs use a combination: temperature + top-p + frequency penalty.

The KV cache:

Generating token t requires attending to all previous tokens 1..t-1. Naively, this means re-computing the Key and Value vectors for all previous tokens at every step — O(n²) total for generating n tokens.

PhD ▼▼ Module 05

PhD Track

The generation process can be formalised as sequential sampling from a conditional distribution:

x_t ~ P_θ(· | x_{<t}) = softmax(W_e · h_t^L + b)

Where h_t^L is the hidden state at position t from the final transformer layer L, and W_e is the unembedding matrix (often the transpose of the embedding matrix, a technique called weight tying).

Temperature as entropy control:

Temperature scaling modifies the logits z before softmax:

P(x_t = v) = exp(z_v / T) / ∑_w exp(z_w / T)

As T → 0, the distribution collapses to a point mass on the argmax (greedy decoding). As T → ∞, the distribution approaches uniform. The entropy of the distribution:

H(T) = -∑_v P_T(v) log P_T(v)

is monotonically increasing in T. Low temperature = low entropy = predictable. High temperature = high entropy = diverse.

Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023):

A technique to speed up generation using a small "draft" model that generates k candidate tokens quickly, then the large model verifies them in a single forward pass (which CAN process multiple tokens in parallel, unlike generation). If the large model agrees with the draft, all k tokens are accepted in one step. If not, generation falls back to the standard autoregressive process at the first disagreement.

Expected speedup: 2-3× for well-matched draft/target model pairs, with ZERO change in output quality (the distribution is mathematically identical).

Module 06: Hallucinations & Limitations

🧠 Ron Burgundy (Anchorman): reads *anything* on the autocue with total confidence — fluently, cheerfully wrong.

❓ Why does it make stuff up?

The model doesn't know what's true — it knows what's *likely*. When it doesn't have enough information, it generates the most plausible-sounding completion, and plausible-sounding is not the same as correct.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 06

Plain English

Remember from Module 01: the model predicts the next word based on patterns. It has never verified a fact, checked a database, or confirmed anything with anyone. It's pattern-matching on language.

When you ask "What was our revenue in Q3 2024?" the model doesn't look up your financial records. It generates text that sounds like a plausible answer to that type of question. If it happens to produce "$2.4M" — that might be right (because it's a plausible-sounding number), or it might be completely wrong.

The confidence problem: The model sounds equally confident whether it's right or wrong. There's no uncertainty indicator. A correct answer and a hallucinated answer look exactly the same. This is the single most dangerous thing about LLMs for professional use.

When hallucinations are most likely:

  • Specific facts (names, dates, numbers, account details)
  • Recent events (training data has a cutoff date)
  • Niche domains (less training data = more guessing)
  • When you ask for something that doesn't exist ("summarise the 2024 ISO standard on AI governance" — if it doesn't exist, the model will invent it)

When hallucinations are least likely:

  • General knowledge and common patterns
  • Structural tasks (formatting, summarising text you've provided)
  • Creative tasks (there's no "wrong" answer)
  • Tasks where you've provided all the source material in the prompt
TECHNICAL ▼ Module 06

Technical

Hallucination occurs when the model's output is fluent and confident but factually incorrect or fabricated. It's a fundamental consequence of the training objective:

The model was trained to minimise:

L(θ) = -∑_t log P_θ(x_t | x_{<t})

This objective rewards predicting likely continuations, not true continuations. The model has no mechanism for distinguishing between text it saw that was factual vs text it saw that was fictional, opinioned, or wrong. It learned patterns from ALL of it.

PhD ▼▼ Module 06

PhD Track

Hallucination can be understood through the lens of calibration and epistemic uncertainty.

A perfectly calibrated model would assign probability p to outcomes that are correct p% of the time. LLMs are generally overconfident — they assign high probability to specific outputs even when the model has insufficient information to be confident.

Why?

  1. Training data bias: The training corpus is overwhelmingly assertive text. Wikipedia, textbooks, and articles state facts confidently. The model learns that assertive text is high-probability text, regardless of whether the assertions are correct.
  1. RLHF bias: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback rewards responses that humans rate as helpful. Confident, specific answers are rated as more helpful than hedged, uncertain ones — even when the hedging is more honest. This creates a systematic bias toward confident outputs.
  1. Softmax saturation: At inference time, the softmax function tends to produce peaked distributions over the vocabulary. Once the model has committed to a particular phrasing direction (e.g., "The budget is $"), the subsequent token distribution for the dollar amount is spread across plausible values, but none of the plausible values are distinguished as "I actually know this" vs "I'm guessing."

Module 07: Prompt Fundamentals

🧠 The Genie / three wishes: you get what you *literally* ask for — word it carefully, mind the monkey's paw.

❓ What makes a good prompt?

A good prompt reduces ambiguity. The less the model has to guess, the better the output.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 07

Plain English

Think about how you'd give instructions to a brilliant new person on their first day.

Bad instruction: "Can you do something about the Johnson account?"

What's wrong with this? The new person doesn't know:

  • Which Johnson? (there might be several)
  • What needs doing? (report? email? meeting prep?)
  • When is it due?
  • What's the context? (are they happy? upset? overdue?)
  • What does "done" look like?

They'll either do the wrong thing confidently, or come back with 10 questions. Either way, you've wasted time.

Good instruction: "The Johnson meeting is at 2pm Thursday. They've been waiting a week for the revised proposal. Pull the latest numbers from the shared drive, draft a one-page summary with the three options we discussed, and flag if anything's changed since last week. I'll review before it goes out."

Same task. Now the new person knows exactly what to do. That's prompt engineering — you're briefing the AI the way you'd brief a competent new colleague.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 07

The Real Insight: Prompts Are Short When Context Is Deep

Most prompt engineering advice gives you long, complex templates. That misses the point.

A long prompt means your context is thin. You're trying to cram everything — role, background, standards, format, constraints — into every single request.

A short prompt means your context is rich. Your base layer handles the permanent stuff (who you are, how you work, what matters). Your prompt only needs to handle the task.

WITHOUT base layer:
"You are a project manager at a commercial construction firm
in Melbourne. You write direct, factual correspondence. Your
clients are developers and architects who prefer bullet points
and hate waffle. Write a progress update for the Smith St
project. Include timeline status, budget status, and next
milestone. Keep it under 200 words, professional tone,
DD/MM/YYYY date format."

→ 70 words of context + 30 words of task

WITH base layer already loaded:
"Progress update for Smith St — timeline, budget, next
milestone."

→ 10 words. Same quality output.

The base layer does 80% of the work. The prompt does 20%.

This is the skill: don't learn to write better prompts. Learn to build a better base layer. Then your prompts can be simple.

Technical

Prompt quality is about reducing the entropy of the model's output distribution.

From Module 05, the model generates from P(x_t | context). A vague context produces a high-entropy distribution (many plausible outputs, most of them wrong for your purpose). A specific context produces a low-entropy distribution (few plausible outputs, most of them useful).

The five dimensions of prompt specificity:

Each dimension you specify narrows the output distribution. All five together produce a tightly constrained, high-quality output.

PhD ▼▼ Module 07

PhD Track

Prompt engineering can be understood through an information-theoretic lens. The mutual information between the prompt X and the desired output Y determines the quality ceiling:

I(X; Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X)

Where H(Y) is the entropy of the desired output space and H(Y|X) is the conditional entropy given the prompt. Maximising I(X;Y) — the information the prompt provides about the desired output — is the fundamental objective.

A perfectly specified prompt would make H(Y|X) ≈ 0 (given the prompt, there's essentially one correct output). A completely vague prompt leaves H(Y|X) ≈ H(Y) (the prompt tells the model nothing).

The curse of implicit constraints:

Human communication relies heavily on shared context and pragmatic inference (Grice's maxims). When you say "do something about Johnson" to a colleague, years of shared context fills in the gaps. The model has none of this shared context — every implicit constraint must be made explicit.

Unless you have a base layer. The base layer IS the shared context. It's the equivalent of those years of working together, compressed into 200 words. With a base layer in place, even a short prompt like "prep me for the Johnson meeting" carries rich meaning — because the AI already knows who you are, what you do, and what "prep" means in your world.

Module 09: The Three Context Levels

❓ What's the difference between raw, dump, and distilled?

The same question produces dramatically different results depending on how much — and how well — you tell the model about your situation. Raw gets you generic. Document dump gets you messy. Distilled gets you professional.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 09

Plain English

"You didn't just give it ingredients — you gave it a recipe. 'Here's the situation, here's what matters, here's what I need.' The AI just follows the recipe."

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 09

Technical

"The distilled prompt maximises mutual information I(prompt; desired_output) while minimising token count. Every element directly constrains the output. Entropy H(output | prompt) is minimised — very few plausible completions satisfy all constraints, and they're all high-quality. Combined with the base layer's persistent context, the effective information density per token is an order of magnitude higher than a document dump."

PhD ▼▼ Module 09

PhD Track

"The distilled prompt approximates a sufficient statistic for the desired output — given the distilled prompt, the raw documents provide negligible additional information: I(raw_docs; desired_output | distilled_prompt) ≈ 0. The distillation step (human reading → extraction → structuring → judgment) is a lossy compression that preserves task-relevant information while discarding noise. This is the step that no amount of model capability can fully automate, because the relevance function is defined by the user's professional judgment, not by the text statistics."

Technical

Few-shot prompting (Brown et al., 2020) provides examples of input-output pairs in the prompt, enabling the model to perform in-context learning — adapting its behaviour based on the examples without any weight updates.

The mechanism is believed to involve the attention mechanism implementing a form of gradient descent at inference time (Oswald et al., 2023). The model's attention layers effectively "learn" the mapping from input to output by attending to the examples.

Number of examples:

  • 0-shot: No examples (rely on instructions only)
  • 1-shot: One example (often sufficient for format matching)
  • 3-5 shot: Multiple examples (needed for complex or ambiguous tasks)
  • Beyond 5: Diminishing returns, token cost increases

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 11

Technical

Chain of thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022) works because it forces the model to generate intermediate reasoning tokens before the final answer. Due to autoregressive generation (Module 05), these intermediate tokens become part of the context for the answer — they constrain the answer to be consistent with the reasoning.

In effect, CoT converts a single-step prediction (hard) into a multi-step prediction (easier per step):

Without CoT: P(answer | question) — one difficult prediction
With CoT:    P(step_1 | question) × P(step_2 | step_1, question) × ... × P(answer | steps, question)
             — many easier predictions

Each intermediate step is a simpler prediction that the model handles well. The final answer is conditioned on all the reasoning, which dramatically reduces errors.

When to use it:

  • Multi-factor decisions ("should we switch suppliers?")
  • Comparisons ("which option is better for this situation?")
  • Calculations ("what's the total cost of ownership?")
  • Troubleshooting ("why is this metric trending down?")

When NOT to use it:

  • Simple formatting tasks
  • Drafting text where the reasoning isn't the point
  • Quick lookups

Few-shot prompting (Brown et al., 2020) provides examples of input-output pairs in the prompt, enabling the model to perform in-context learning — adapting its behaviour based on the examples without any weight updates.

PhD ▼▼ Module 11

PhD Track

CoT prompting exploits the model's ability to perform serial computation through autoregressive generation. Without CoT, the model must compute the answer in a single forward pass — a fixed depth of computation bounded by the number of transformer layers. With CoT, each generated token adds another forward pass, effectively giving the model unbounded computational depth.

This connects to the theory of chain of thought as computation (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023): transformer decoders with CoT are Turing complete, while single-pass transformers are limited to TC^0 (constant-depth threshold circuits). The intermediate tokens are the model's "scratchpad" — working memory that extends its computational capacity.

The empirical effectiveness of CoT scales with model size. Below ~10B parameters, CoT provides minimal benefit. Above ~100B parameters, CoT unlocks qualitatively new capabilities (emergent reasoning). This suggests that CoT accesses latent capabilities that exist in the weights but aren't accessible through single-step prediction.

Module 13: Privacy, Ethics & What Not To Share

🧠 The wartime 'careless talk costs lives' poster: mind what you feed it — not everything stays private.

❓ What should I never put in a prompt?

Anything you type into an AI tool may be stored, logged, or used for training. Treat the prompt box like a public notice board — don't put anything on it you wouldn't want seen.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 13

Plain English

When you type something into Copilot or the company GPT, that text goes to a server somewhere. Even if the company promises it's private, you're creating a record that didn't exist before. If there's ever a data breach, a legal discovery request, or an audit — those prompts and responses may be discoverable.

Think of it like email: you wouldn't email a client's medical history to a random address. Don't paste it into an AI tool either.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 13

Technical

LLM interactions create data artefacts at multiple levels:

  1. Prompt logging: Most AI providers log input/output pairs for abuse detection, quality monitoring, and debugging. Enterprise agreements may limit this, but logs exist.
  1. Context persistence: Within a conversation, all prior messages are included in the context window. If you paste Client A's data in message 3 and ask about Client B in message 5, Client A's data is still in the context — and could influence the response about Client B (cross-contamination).
  1. Training data feedback: Free-tier tools may use conversations for model improvement. Even "opted out" enterprise tools may have edge cases in their data processing pipelines.
  1. Caching and CDN artefacts: Generated responses may be cached at various infrastructure levels. While typically short-lived, they create transient copies.

Best practices:

  • Use separate conversations for separate clients
  • Clear the conversation when switching between sensitive topics
  • Anonymise by default — use real names only when the tool is confirmed-safe
  • Never paste complete documents — extract only what's needed
PhD ▼▼ Module 13

PhD Track

The privacy challenge with LLMs is fundamentally a data flow control problem. Traditional data governance assumes data flows through defined channels with clear retention and access controls. LLM interactions create ad-hoc data flows that bypass these channels:

Traditional: Client data → CRM (access-controlled) → report (access-controlled)
With LLM:    Client data → prompt (logged externally) → response (logged) → report

The "externally logged" step introduces a data copy outside the organization's direct control. Even with enterprise agreements, the legal and regulatory implications of this copy are still being litigated globally (GDPR, Privacy Act 1988, CCPA).

Model memorisation: Research has shown that LLMs can memorise and regurgitate training data verbatim (Carlini et al., 2021). If client data enters a training pipeline — even indirectly — it could theoretically be extracted by adversarial prompting. The probability is low for any specific data point, but the risk is non-zero and the consequences for professional services (where data includes financial, personal, and sensitive business information) are severe.

Differential privacy approaches (adding calibrated noise to training data) reduce but don't eliminate memorisation risk. Most current commercial LLMs do NOT use differential privacy in training.

Module 16: Model Choices — Not All AI Is the Same

🧠 A golf bag of clubs: driver for distance, putter for precision — pick the right one for the shot.

❓ What's the difference between GPT, Claude, Copilot, and all these models?

Different AI models are like different colleagues — some are fast and cheap, some are deep thinkers, some are specialists. Knowing which one to use for which task is a real skill.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 16

Plain English

Think of it like cars. A Toyota Corolla and a Tesla and a truck all have engines and wheels, but they're built for different things. The Corolla is reliable and cheap. The Tesla is fast and smooth. The truck carries heavy loads.

AI models are the same. They all do "predict the next word" but they differ in:

  • How big they are (more parameters = more capable, but slower and more expensive)
  • What they were trained on (some saw more code, some more conversation, some more documents)
  • How they were fine-tuned (some optimised for helpfulness, some for safety, some for reasoning)
  • How fast they run (bigger models = slower = more expensive per query)

You don't need to know the details. You just need to know: the tool you use matters, and different tools suit different tasks.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 16

The Brain Analogy (from Module 00)

We said LLMs are like brains. Here's the thing: not all brains are the same.

Some people are quick and practical — ask them a question, get an answer in 5 seconds. Great for everyday stuff. Not who you'd ask to design a building.

Some people are slow and thorough — they think deeply, consider angles you'd miss, come back with something brilliant. But they take time, and you wouldn't ask them to fire off 20 quick emails.

Some people are specialists — incredible at one thing (writing code, analysing data, creative work) and average at everything else.

AI models are the same. They vary in speed, depth, cost, and speciality. The model behind Copilot is not the same as the model behind ChatGPT which is not the same as Claude. And even within one company, there are tiers.

---

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 16

Technical

Model differences come from four axes:

1. Architecture and scale

  • Parameter count: ranges from 7B (Gemma) to 1.8T+ (GPT-4)
  • More parameters = more capacity to encode patterns = generally better quality
  • But: diminishing returns. 2× parameters ≠ 2× quality

2. Training data

  • Volume: trillions of tokens for frontier models
  • Composition: ratio of code, conversation, documents, books, web text
  • Recency: training data cutoff determines what the model "knows"
  • Quality: filtered, deduplicated, curated data → better model behaviour

3. Post-training / alignment

  • RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback): teaches the model to be helpful, harmless, and honest
  • Constitutional AI (Anthropic's approach): trains the model against a set of principles
  • Instruction tuning: fine-tunes on instruction/response pairs
  • These shape the model's "personality" — how it responds to requests, handles ambiguity, admits uncertainty

4. Inference optimisation

  • Quantisation: reducing precision of weights (16-bit → 8-bit → 4-bit) to run faster with some quality loss
  • Distillation: training a smaller model to mimic a larger one
  • Speculative decoding: using a small fast model to draft, large model to verify

PhD ▼▼ Module 16

PhD Track

The performance differences between models can be understood through the scaling laws framework (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022 "Chinchilla"):

L(N, D) = A/N^α + B/D^β + E

Where N is parameter count, D is training data size, α ≈ 0.076, β ≈ 0.095, and E is irreducible loss. This predicts that:

  • Optimal performance requires balancing N and D (the "Chinchilla-optimal" frontier)
  • For a given compute budget C, there's an optimal (N, D) pair
  • Frontier labs have converged on similar parameter counts because the scaling laws constrain the design space

The post-training gap:

Two models with identical architecture and training data can produce dramatically different outputs due to post-training. RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) reshape the output distribution to align with human preferences. The base model's capability sets the ceiling; post-training determines how much of that capability is accessible via natural language instructions.

Mixture of Experts (MoE):

Some models (GPT-4 reportedly, Mixtral explicitly) use MoE architectures where only a subset of parameters activate for each token. A 1.8T parameter MoE model might only activate ~200B parameters per forward pass, making it cheaper to run while maintaining high total capacity. This explains how models can be both "very large" and "reasonably fast."

Module 17: RAG & Search — When the AI Needs to Look Things Up

🧠 The open-book exam: closed book = memory only; open book = you may bring your notes.

❓ How does the AI know about MY company's data?

The model's knowledge is frozen at its training cutoff — it knows nothing about your company, your documents, or anything that happened after training ended. RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) fixes this by retrieving relevant information first, then handing it to the model as context before it answers.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 17

Plain English

Imagine you've hired a brilliant new analyst. They're smart, great at writing, excellent at summarising — but they've never worked at your company before. They don't know your systems, your clients, your internal processes.

You can do one of two things:

Option A — Fine-tuning: Spend months training them. Make them read every document, memorise the policies, internalise everything. At the end, they've absorbed it all. But it's slow, expensive, and the moment anything changes, they're out of date again.

Option B — RAG: Before every meeting, hand them the relevant files from the filing cabinet. They read the relevant bits, answer your question drawing on those files, and cite what they used. No lengthy training period. The cabinet updates itself. They're always current.

RAG is Option B. Instead of baking company knowledge into the model, you fetch relevant documents at the time of the question and put them in front of the model as context.

This is why your company's AI tool can discuss your internal policies without the model ever having "learned" them.

Technical

RAG is a pipeline with two distinct phases: retrieval and generation.

The retrieval pipeline:

The vector database:

Documents are pre-processed (chunked, embedded) and stored in a vector database. At query time, the query is embedded using the same embedding model, and the database finds the chunks whose vectors are closest to the query vector — "closest" meaning most semantically similar.

This is why RAG can find relevant information even when the exact words don't match. "What's our policy on expense reimbursement?" retrieves chunks about "staff claims," "out-of-pocket costs," and "approval thresholds" — because they're semantically close, even without the exact word "reimbursement."

PhD ▼▼ Module 17

PhD Track

RAG was formalised by Lewis et al. (2020) in "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (NeurIPS 2020). The original formulation treats RAG as a latent variable model:

p(y | x) = ∑_z p_η(z | x) · p_θ(y | x, z)

Where:

  • x is the input query
  • z are the retrieved documents (latent variable)
  • p_η is the retriever (e.g., a bi-encoder)
  • p_θ is the generator (the LLM)

The retriever and generator can be jointly fine-tuned or kept separate (the more common production pattern is a frozen retriever + frozen LLM, with engineering effort on the pipeline itself).

Dense vs sparse retrieval:

MethodMechanismStrengthsWeaknesses
BM25 (sparse)TF-IDF variant — keyword frequency/rarityFast, interpretable, no GPU requiredLexical match only — misses synonyms and paraphrase
Bi-encoder (dense)Separate encoders for query and document, cosine similaritySemantic match, handles paraphraseRequires embedding model, more compute
Cross-encoderQuery and document encoded jointly through attentionHighest accuracy, captures full interactionO(n) per query — cannot pre-index, too slow for retrieval

Production systems typically use dense retrieval (bi-encoder) for the initial recall pass, then a cross-encoder re-ranker to improve precision on the top-k candidates. The cross-encoder only processes the top 20-50 candidates, making it tractable.

Module 18: Agents and Automation — When AI Does Things, Not Just Talks

🧠 R2-D2: rolls off on its own, *does* the task, comes back — a brain with hands.

❓ Can AI actually DO things, or does it just write text?

An AI agent is a model that doesn't just respond — it takes actions, checks the results, and takes more actions, looping until a task is done.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 18

Plain English

Think of a very capable assistant. When you ask them to "organise the travel for the offsite," they don't come back to you with a list of options and wait for you to make each decision. They book flights, reserve accommodation, send calendar invites, and email the team — then they put a summary on your desk and say "here's what I did, just check the dates." That's an agent. The AI version does the same thing, except the tools it calls are web searches, calendar APIs, and email systems.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 18

The Brain Analogy

Every module so far has treated the AI like an advisor. You ask a question. It gives you an answer. You take the answer away and do something with it.

That's useful. But it's not the whole picture.

Think about the difference between a colleague who tells you what to do and a colleague who actually does it. The first one is a consultant — smart, but you still have to do the work. The second one is an assistant — you hand them a task and it gets done.

Until recently, AI was entirely in the consultant category. You'd ask "draft me an email to the client," it would write the text, and you'd copy it into Outlook and press send yourself.

Agents change this. An agent doesn't just tell you what to do. It does it. It opens Outlook, drafts the email, attaches the right file, and — if you've given it permission — sends it. Or it stops just before sending and asks you to confirm. That last bit matters a lot.

---

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 18

Technical

Agents extend the base LLM by providing it with a set of callable tools (function definitions) and an orchestration loop. The model is prompted with the task plus the available tools. At each step, the model either generates a tool call (structured JSON specifying function name and arguments) or a text response. Tool calls are intercepted by the application layer, executed against real systems, and the results are appended to the context. The model then generates the next step.

Tool calls are structured as function signatures with typed parameters:

{
  "tool": "search_web",
  "arguments": {
    "query": "Acme Corp Q1 earnings 2025",
    "max_results": 5
  }
}

The application layer executes this, returns a result object, and the model receives:

[Tool result: search_web]
1. Acme Corp reports Q1 revenue of $42M, up 12% YoY...
2. ...

The model then reasons over the result and decides whether to call another tool or produce a final response. Modern agent frameworks (LangChain, LlamaIndex, AutoGen, Microsoft Semantic Kernel) handle the loop infrastructure so developers write tool definitions, not orchestration logic.

Memory: Agents need memory across steps. Short-term memory lives in the context window (all tool results accumulate there). Long-term memory is handled externally — a database or vector store the agent can read and write across sessions.

PhD ▼▼ Module 18

PhD Track

ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) formalises the reasoning + acting framework as an interleaved sequence:

(Thought_1, Action_1, Observation_1, Thought_2, Action_2, Observation_2, ..., Answer)

The key insight is that explicit reasoning traces improve action selection: the model's thought is conditioned on prior observations, and subsequent actions are conditioned on the thought. This outperforms action-only approaches (which hallucinate chains) and reasoning-only approaches (which can't ground in real-world feedback).

Planning beyond ReAct — Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023b):

ReAct is a linear chain. Tree of Thoughts (ToT) extends this to a tree search over reasoning paths. The model generates multiple candidate thoughts at each step, evaluates them (via self-evaluation or external scoring), and expands the most promising. This allows backtracking — a critical feature for complex multi-step tasks where greedy decoding leads to dead ends.

Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023):

A self-improvement mechanism for agents. After a failed task attempt, the model generates a verbal reflection — identifying what went wrong and how to correct it. This reflection is stored and prepended to future attempts, acting as an episodic memory that prevents the agent from repeating the same mistakes. Reflexion agents significantly outperform ReAct on tasks requiring multi-step reasoning and recovery from errors.

Module 19: The Future & Trajectory — Where Is This All Going?

🧠 The DeLorean (Back to the Future): where we're going — and how fast the road is changing.

❓ Is this just hype, or is my job about to change?

This is real, it is accelerating, and it will change how you work — but not in the "robots replace everyone" way, and the people who thrive will be the ones who learned to work with it rather than wait to see what happens.
PLAIN ENGLISH Module 19

Plain English

Think about what happened to photography when digital cameras arrived. Professional photographers did not disappear — but the job changed completely. The darkroom technicians whose whole job was developing film largely did disappear. The photographers who could compose a great shot, understand light, build a relationship with a subject — they are still in high demand.

AI is that shift for knowledge work. The "darkroom" parts of your job — reformatting documents, writing boilerplate, summarising information you have already read, filling in templates — these are the tasks that change first and fastest. The judgment, the relationships, the domain knowledge, the decisions only you can make: these are the composition skills. They go up in value as the darkroom disappears.

The question to ask about your own role is not "will AI replace me?" It is "which parts of my job are darkroom work, and which parts are composition?" The darkroom is going away. Build your composition skills.

TECHNICAL ▼ Module 19

Technical

The trajectory of LLM capability can be understood through three lenses:

Scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022): Model performance scales predictably with compute, data, and parameters. We are not near the ceiling. The frontier labs have visibility into what current investment in compute will produce 18-24 months out, and the trajectory is consistent. This is why internal roadmaps at major labs extend years into the future with reasonable confidence.

Emergent capabilities: As models scale, qualitatively new capabilities appear at unpredictable thresholds — abilities that were absent at smaller scale and appear abruptly at larger scale (Wei et al., 2022). Multi-step reasoning, in-context learning, and code generation all emerged this way. The implication: capability jumps are non-linear. The next threshold may produce capabilities that current models do not hint at.

Post-training improvements: Pre-training (predicting the next token on internet text) is one part of the story. RLHF, Constitutional AI, instruction tuning, and reasoning-focused training are all improving the usable capability of models independently of raw scale. The gap between what a model knows and what it reliably delivers is being closed through better alignment techniques. This means current frontier models likely have substantial untapped capability being progressively unlocked through training methods, not just parameter count.

PhD ▼▼ Module 19

PhD Track

Scaling laws and their implications:

Kaplan et al. (2020) established that loss scales as a power law in N (parameters), D (data), and C (compute):

L(N, D) ≈ (Nc/N)^(αN) + (Dc/D)^(αD) + Lmin

Hoffmann et al. (2022) — the "Chinchilla" paper — refined this to show that previous frontier models were substantially undertrained: for a given compute budget, optimal performance requires scaling data and parameters together at roughly a 20:1 token-to-parameter ratio. This corrected the field's prior bias toward large models with insufficient data, and produced models like Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) that outperformed much larger but undertrained models.

The implication for trajectory: the scaling laws have not broken. The frontier has largely converged on Chinchilla-optimal or near-optimal training runs. Further capability improvements will come from both continued scaling and from architectural innovations (mixture of experts, state-space models, new attention mechanisms) that improve performance per FLOP.

Module 21

Thinking Like a Power User

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 21

The one shift everything hangs on

A normal user asks: "How do I do this task?"

A power user asks: *"What kind* of problem is this — and what system handles

that kind?"**

That's the entire difference. Not more tools, not more shortcuts — a different

question asked at the start. Normal users do the work. Power users

direct the work. Everything below is just that shift, made concrete.

You are not trying to turn your audience into programmers. You are trying to

change the first question they ask when a task lands on their desk.

---

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 21

The Power-User Reflex — six questions

Before touching a task, a power user runs a silent checklist. Teach these six

questions as a reflex. This is the takeaway to put on the wall.

1. Have I done this before? → automate it
2. Is this a one-off, or a pattern? → build the reusable version
3. Is this data wearing a costume? → extract / transform it
4. What output would save the most clicks? → ask for that file
5. Can I make the machine check me? → validate
6. Can I describe the result and let it find the steps? → delegate the how
Not to be confused with: Module 10's Prompt Blueprint six questions, which brief a single task. The Reflex runs first — it decides what kind of problem this is and whether a tool should handle it. The Blueprint runs second — it briefs the task you've decided to delegate. Reflex chooses the battle; Blueprint fights it.

The rest of this module is twenty concrete reframes, grouped under the question

each one answers. They're not a list to memorise — they're evidence that the

six questions work on almost anything.

---

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 21

Question 1 — "Have I done this before?" → automate it

The tell: you feel the boredom of repetition. That boredom is a signal.

  • If you've done it twice, stop doing it by hand. Renaming 50 files, saving

30 attachments → "write me a script to do all of these at once."

  • Batch-personalise. 20 renewal reminders, each slightly different →

template plus a list, not 20 blank pages. *Personalisation is a merge, not a

marathon.*

  • Name things so future-you wins. "Rename these to

YYYY-MM-DD_client_type.pdf." A naming convention is a tiny system that pays

off forever.

  • Standardise the mess. Five people, five date formats → "clean and

standardise this list." Impose consistency by machine, not by nagging.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 21

Question 2 — "One-off, or a pattern?" → build the reusable version

The tell: you're about to solve something you'll face again next month.

  • Make a template from your best work. One great proposal → "turn this into

a reusable template with placeholders." A good one-off is a wasted asset until

you templatise it.

  • Tool the recurring annoyance. That fiddly weekly task → "make me a small

HTML or Excel tool that does it." If it annoys you on a schedule, build for it

once.

  • Turn words into a diagram. Describe a process → get a flowchart you reuse

every time you onboard someone. If you can say it, you can draw it.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 21

Question 3 — "Is this data wearing a costume?" → extract / transform it

*The tell: information is trapped in a format you can't work with — a PDF, an

image, an email, a wall of text.*

  • That screenshot is data. A photo of a whiteboard, a scan, a portal

screenshot → "extract this into a table," don't retype it.

  • Your folder is a database. "Across these 10 policy PDFs, which exclude

flood?" Stop opening files one by one — query them.

  • Convert, never recreate. Notes → slides, PDF → editable table, transcript

→ action list. Format is fluid; never rebuild what you can transform.

  • Give one example, get a hundred. Paste one correctly-formatted record →

"make 20 more like this." Examples teach patterns; patterns scale.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 21

Question 4 — "What output would save the most clicks?" → ask for the file

*The tell: you're about to copy text off the screen and paste it somewhere.

Stop — ask for the finished artefact instead.*

  • Ask for the file, not the text. Calendar events → .ics. A mess of

details → .csv. A chart → .svg. A little tool → .html. *If a computer

can read the format, it can write it.*

  • Generate the boring calc. "Write the formula / DAX / regex that does

this." Never do by hand what you can specify.

  • Decode anything scary. An error message, a legal clause, an acronym-soup

email → "explain in plain English and tell me what to do." Nothing is above

your pay grade to understand.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 21

Question 5 — "Can I make the machine check me?" → validate

*The tell: you're about to send, commit, or decide something that would be

costly to get wrong.*

  • Ask what could go wrong first. Before you send → "what am I missing,

what could a client misread, what are the edge cases?" Pre-mortem, not

post-mortem.

  • Let it check your work. "Review this for tone, errors, missing info, and

anything ambiguous." A second set of eyes is now free and instant.

  • Always verify the extraction. When it pulls numbers out of documents,

trust nothing until you've spot-checked. The machine is confident, not

correct.

PLAIN ENGLISH Module 21

Question 6 — "Can I describe the result and let it find the steps?" → delegate the how

*The tell: you're about to micromanage — spelling out every step. Describe the

destination instead and let it navigate.*

  • Describe the outcome, not the steps. "A one-page summary a busy exec can

skim — you decide the structure." Delegate the how; own the what.

  • Make it interview you. Handover doc, SOP, scope → "ask me questions one at

a time until you can write it." Pull the knowledge out; don't stare at a blank

page.

  • Externalise the decision. Torn between options → "build a weighted scoring

table: these 4 options against these 5 criteria." Turn a fuzzy feeling into a

visible structure.

  • Rubber-duck the hard problem. Stuck → "I'm trying to figure out X — ask me

questions to help me think it through." It's a thinking partner, not an answer

vending machine.

  • Ask the tool to level you up. "What's a better way I could've asked that?

What can you do that I'm probably not using?" Interrogate your own tool use —

the meta-skill that makes all the others compound.
