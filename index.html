A working session

AI at work: what it is, and how to actually use it.

A plain-English look at the AI assistants turning up in our tools — what they actually are, what makes their answers genuinely useful, and how to put them to work in your day.

The spine of the whole session is a single predictor answering one specialty question. We meet it early giving a confident, slightly-wrong general-knowledge answer, then we add one layer of context at a time and watch that same answer climb. At the end we see the whole climb in one view. Keep pointing at the question, it never changes; only the context does.

Runs offline once loaded, press S for speaker view, click the coloured widgets live. The title slide runs an ambient looping animation (particles + a token travelling a small network) meant to play while people join — it pauses automatically once you move past this slide.

What today is about

Three simple questions, in order.

  • 1.  What actually is this thing you're typing into?
  • 2.  What turns a generic answer into a genuinely useful one?
  • 3.  And how do you get genuinely useful work out of it?

No jargon, no maths. You'll leave able to use these tools well, not to build one.

Orient them in ten seconds: not a technical deep-dive, not a sales pitch for AI. Three plain questions, answered in order, each one earns the next. By the end they'll be more effective with the tools already on their desk. Reveal the three with the arrow key so it doesn't dump at once.

Act 1 · training
three stages

Three tabs, three jobs. Don't rush, this is the mental model for the whole session. Step 1 now does double duty: the next-word game AND the "weight" forming (the confidence bar climbs as the pile keeps repeating the pattern). Point at the bar, that bar IS a weight, and a strong weight IS a fact it knows. Land the takeaway: it's all general insurance, your specialty is barely in there.

The pretraining loop, in plain words (say this on Step 1): the model makes a prediction, guesses the next word. Then it checks against the real word and, if it got it wrong, it goes back and slightly adjusts itself so it's a bit more likely to be right next time. That "go back and fix the mistake" step is called back-propagation, don't labour the term, but it's just the model correcting itself. Then it does the whole thing again. And again. Trillions of times.

Land the human analogy, it makes the whole thing click for a lay room: this is basically how we learn too. You try something, you get it right or wrong, you notice the mistake, and you adjust. Do that enough times and it becomes second nature, you just "know" it. Same here: no one hands the model facts, it gets them right or wrong billions of times, fixes its mistakes, and the patterns that survive become what it knows.

Step 2: reveal the request/answer pairs, this is what turns a text-continuer into something that answers. Step 3: let the room pick the better of two answers, that's where tone and duty of care come from. All insurance examples. Tabs are clickable; you don't have to run all three deeply if time is short.

Act 1 · how it processes
one word, top to bottom

This is the structural version of the "predict, pick, repeat" slide right after it — that one shows the behaviour, this one shows what's actually inside the box doing it. Step through with the arrow key: the dot travels down and the current stage lights up. Reverse works too, the dot walks back up.

Real terms are shown small on each stage on purpose — if someone in the room already knows "self-attention" or "softmax", this doesn't talk down to them, it just adds the plain-English reading next to it.

Router/Experts step (this is the modern part, a mixture-of-experts model): older models pushed every word through one fixed feedforward block. Most large models today instead have several specialist sub-networks ("experts") and a small router that, for each individual word, picks which one or two specialists should actually handle it. The dot visibly jumps to just one expert — the other three stay dim because they genuinely don't run for this word. That's the punchline to land: a model can have an enormous number of parameters in total and still be fast per word, because any single word only ever activates a small slice of it.

So what actually is "context"?

The model on its own knows only general things. Context is the extra information you hand it alongside your question, and you add it in layers, each one narrowing from "any business" toward "this exact client".

One catch, and the skill it demands: dirty vs clean context

Its working memory is finite. "Clean" doesn\u2019t mean empty, it means everything you give it is relevant, and the junk is left out. This question needs it to compare two documents \u2014 the quote and the application \u2014 so both have to stay in view.

Clean chat · relevant stuff, well organised
Dirty chat · everything, buried in junk
Q: "Are there any gaps between this quote and what the client declared in their application?"

To spot the gap, the model has to hold the quote and the application side by side. The clean chat kept both in view; the dirty chat lost the application under the junk, so it never made the comparison. Curated beats crammed.

Act 2 · the limit
dirty vs clean context

This is a real broker question, spotting a gap between the quote and the application, and it only works if the model can see both documents at once. In the clean chat both are present and it catches that the declared staff aren\u2019t on the quote. In the dirty chat the application got buried under a 40-page wording, holiday photos and a lunch chat, dropped out of view, so the model never compared the two and waves the quote through.

Make the contrast clear: the clean chat is NOT empty, it\u2019s a tidy pack where every item earns its place. The dirty chat holds the same application, but drowned in junk it falls out of view. Curated beats crammed. Whoever gives it a clean relevant pack beats whoever dumps everything plus their lunch order.

Act 3 · the plumbing

Answers the obvious question after the climb, "do I rebuild that pack every time?" No. The industry and product rungs barely change between clients, write them once and every job starts most of the way up. Only the client rung is fresh. End on the compounding line: the tool everyone's given is identical, the edge is the pack you bring.

Three things to take with you
  • Context beats raw knowledge, almost always. Feed it what it needs to know.
  • Feed it the right thing, not everything, its memory is finite.
  • Treat the first answer as a draft. If it sounds surer than it should be, check it.

This week: write one paragraph of context for a task you do often, save it, and paste it in on Monday. That's your library started.

A quick glossary — the words you'll hear
everyday worth knowing
Everyday
Large Language Model (LLM) — the kind of AI behind these tools; trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word.
Prompt — what you type in: your question, plus any instructions and context.
Token — a chunk of text, often part of a word. It reads and counts in these, not letters.
Context window — how much it can hold in mind at once — its working memory for a chat.
Hallucination — when it states something untrue as if it were fact, sounding just as confident.
Training data — the vast pile of text it learned patterns from — not a database it looks things up in.
Fine-tuning — extra training that nudges a model towards a narrower job or house style.
Worth knowing
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) — it looks up relevant documents first, then answers from what it found. How Copilot uses your files.
Grounding — tying an answer to real, cited sources instead of memory — so you can check it.
System prompt — hidden instructions that set how the assistant behaves before you type a word.
Temperature — a randomness dial: low is safe and repeatable, high is more varied and creative.
Embedding — turning text into numbers so a machine can measure how close two things are in meaning.
Agent — an AI that can take steps and use tools to get something done, not just reply.
Multimodal — handles more than text — images, audio, files — going in and coming out.
